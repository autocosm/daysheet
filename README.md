## Tester instructions

Thanks for helping me test DaySheet. It takes about 25 minutes, and you can stop whenever you like. You're testing the design, not yourself. If something confuses you, the design has a problem, not you, and that's exactly what I need to find.

## What DaySheet is

DaySheet is a phone app idea for touring musicians. On the road, the details for each show (venue contact, load-in, soundcheck, set time, hotel, drive time) are scattered across emails, texts, and spreadsheets. DaySheet keeps them in one place for each date of a tour. It shows what's still missing, who needs to confirm what, and what changed. It gives each person only the view they should have: the tour manager sees everything, a bandmate sees the day, and a guest artist or venue contact sees only their own shows.

What you're testing is a clickable prototype, not a finished app. The data is made up, and some things are deliberately simplified.

## Before you start

1. Open the prototype in a browser on a phone or computer: https://autocosm.github.io/daysheet/daysheet-prototype.html
2. Next to the phone-sized screen there is a small control panel. You can use it to switch who you're viewing as and to reset the sample data. If you get lost, use "Reset sample data" and start the task over.
3. Nothing you do is sent to me. Your changes stay in your own browser, so please send me your answers using the form below.
4. Please think out loud, or jot notes as you go. Say what you expect to happen before you tap, and what you thought when it did or didn't.

## Your role

You're the tour manager, Evan, on a short run of shows. You're organized but busy, and you want to know at a glance what needs your attention. Tour managers will recognize the problem: every show has ten small questions, and someone always forgets one.

## Tasks

Do these in order. For each one, you don't need to be fast or "right." If you get stuck for more than a couple of minutes, write down where and why, then move on.

1. First look (2 min). Open the prototype and don't tap anything yet. In your own words, what do you think this is for? What do you notice first? What would you tap first, and why?
2. Showtime. Pretend it's the day of a show. Find out what time the van leaves and when you'll arrive at the next stop.
3. Find the gaps. Pick a show that isn't ready. Work out what's missing and what you'd do about it. Try both adding something yourself and asking someone else for it.
4. Change the plan. Load-in has moved an hour on one show. Make that change. Then find where you or your band could see that something changed.
5. Confirm and send. Get one show to the point where you'd call it confirmed. Then send the day sheet to the band the way you normally would (text or email).
6. See it through someone else's eyes. Switch to Theo, a bandmate who can only view. Try to change the hotel. Then switch to Lena, the venue contact. What can she see, and what can't she? Does it feel right, and is it clear why?
7. Start from scratch (stretch task, 8 min). Use the control panel to clear all data. You've just booked a new run of shows. Build the tour: add a few dates and cities, then add a few details to one of them.

## How to help most

- Say what you're thinking, especially when you're unsure, surprised, or annoyed.
- Try the obvious thing first. Don't hunt for what you think I want you to find.
- If a word, label, or number doesn't match how touring people actually talk or work, tell me. You know the road better than I do, and this is where I need you most.
- Tell me what's missing from a real tour day, even if it isn't in the app.
- It's fine to say "I wouldn't use this" and why.

## What to send back

For each task, send me three things:

- Did you finish it? Easily, with some trouble, or not at all.
- How easy was it? Rate 1 (very difficult) to 7 (very easy).
- What happened? Anything that confused you, surprised you, or felt good, in a sentence or two.

When you've finished, answer these:

1. In your own words, what is DaySheet, and who is it for?
2. What was the most confusing moment?
3. What was the most useful or pleasant moment?
4. What would you need to see before you'd trust this with a real tour?
5. What's missing, or what's wrong for how touring actually works?
6. What device, browser, and screen size did you use? Did the text feel readable?

## Summary

Independent bands manage routing, lodging, venue contacts, schedules, and payouts without professional support staff. Having lived that workflow as a touring musician, I used my Google UX Design Certificate capstone to explore a question I knew firsthand: how might a product free critical tour knowledge trapped in one band member's head? I began by assuming the planner needed better organizing tools. Mapping assumptions and comparing existing approaches exposed a broader problem: everyone needed the same current information, while newer tourers also needed help knowing what should exist at all.
